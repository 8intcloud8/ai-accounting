Course introduction
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/goods-and-services-tax

If you have started a small business or are thinking about it, you may be

wondering if you will need to register for GST. There are rules around GST

registration that you need to be aware of. If the rules apply to you, then

your business must be registered. If the rules don't apply, you can still

register voluntarily if you want to claim GST credits on your business

purchases. Once you register, you need to understand how GST relates to your

sales and business expenses.


What is the Goods and services tax (GST)?
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/module/1290/take

The Goods and services tax (GST) is a broad-based tax of 10% which is applied to most goods, services and other items sold or consumed in Australia.
Most sales in Australia are subject to GST, although there are some exemptions.
Sales that have GST included in their price are known as taxable sales.
How does GST help our community?
The money collected from GST is given to the states and territories where it helps build and support the community. GST helps fund essential services including:
Health cares: hospitals, ambulance services, and public health services such as cancer screening and immunisation
Public safety services: police, fire, and SES, as well as disaster relief in bush fires and floods
Education services: schools, universities, and TAFE
Social services: childcare, aged care, and disability support services
Justice and community safety services: dispute resolution, prisons, road and water safety programs and support for vulnerable children, families and victims of crime
Environmental services: conservation programs to protect our wildlife and its habitat
Infrastructure: building roads, bridges and public transport including buses, trams, trains and ferries
Cultural and sporting institutions: libraries, art galleries, museums, and sports facilities.


Your role as a business owner
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1787

When you register for GST, you will be required to collect GST on behalf of the government and pay it to the ATO.
You collect this extra money by including GST in the price you charge for your goods or services.
You can also claim credits from the ATO for any GST you’ve paid when buying goods and services for your business. These credits will offset any amounts you need to pay.
It’s important to remember that you cannot keep the GST you collect. It is not your money or part of your income.
When you pay the GST amounts you’ve collected on behalf of the government, you're helping to build and support vital services for the Australian community.
The money you collect is distributed by the State Government to help pay for a range of services such as hospitals, schools, childcare, emergency services including police, fire and ambulances, roads and public transport.
Consider the following scenario.
Imagine a situation where a local hospital urgently needs to expand its facilities to accommodate an increase in patients. The GST collected from businesses in the community plays a critical role in funding this expansion.
By collecting and then paying GST to the ATO, businesses are directly contributing to the improvement of healthcare services in their area. This means that when families need medical care, the hospital is equipped to provide it, ultimately benefitting everyone in the community.


GST free items and services
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1788

Whilst most items attract GST, there are some goods and services that are considered GST-free.
Sales that do not include GST in their price are known as GST-free sales. In contrast, sales that have GST included in their price are known as ‘taxable sales’.
Examples of items that are GST-free include:
basic food, such as fruits, vegetables, meat, fish and eggs
medical and other health care services for which a Medicare benefit is payable
some educational services provided by schools, universities and TAFE
goods and services which are exported from Australia
some government charges such as car registration and passport fees.
Working out whether an item attracts GST isn’t always simple.
Consider the following situation.
Let’s say you purchase a pre-made sandwich from a café.
While the bread and basic food ingredients (such as lettuce, tomato and cheese) may be GST-free, the sandwich ‘as a whole’ attracts GST because it is a ‘prepared food’.
When you buy the sandwich, you are paying for more than the individual ingredients. You’re also paying for the labour, packaging and the convenience of having the sandwich prepared and ready to eat.
GST applies to the final price of the sandwich, even though its ingredients are GST-free.
The ATO provides a Detailed food list and a GST food and beverage tool to assist with calculating GST status.
If you are unsure whether an item attracts GST or is GST-free, don’t guess or rely on hearsay from others. Check the ATO website, contact the ATO or speak to your registered tax adviser. They can provide accurate advice and support to help you get things right from the start.

Input-tax sales
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1790

Some goods and services are sold without GST in their price, even though GST was included in the price of the ‘inputs’ used to make or supply them. These sales are known as input-taxed sales.
The most common input-taxed sales are financial supplies (such as lending money or the provision of credit for a fee) and selling or renting out residential premises.
Consider the following example.
Hayley runs an appliance store and sells a fridge to Louise for $1,100 (including $100 GST).
Hayley sells the fridge to Louise on credit and charges interest on the credit.
As providing credit is a financial supply, Hayley cannot include GST on the amount of interest she charges Louise.
Additional information on Input-taxed sales is available on the ATO website.


Income and GST
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1791

You do not record GST on your annual tax return.
GST is a separate tax that you collect for the government. Although you may include GST in your sales, it is not part of your income, and you cannot claim income tax deductions against it.
You report both GST payments and credits on your Business Activity Statement (BAS).


Do I need to register?
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/module/1296/take

You must register for GST:
when your business or enterprise has a GST turnover of $75,000 or more ($150,000 or more for not-for-profit organisations)
if your business is a taxi or ride-sourcing service (for example, Uber, GoCatch, DIDI) regardless of your GST turnover, or
you want to claim fuel tax credits through your business.
The ATO website has additional information on Reporting and paying GST for ride-sourcing services and GST and taxis .
You can also undertake our Ride-sourcing: Income, expenses and GST course.
Registering for GST is your responsibility, and you must monitor your turnover each month to see if you will reach the registration turnover threshold. If you identify that you will reach or exceed the turnover threshold, then you must register for GST within 21 days. This is not a choice. It is required by law.
Voluntary registration
If you don’t meet the criteria above, you can still register for GST, but it’s not compulsory. If you do decide to register for GST and your GST turnover is below the $75,000 threshold you:
will need to include GST in the price of goods and services you sell
can claim GST credits for business purchases you make
will be required to lodge business activity statements.


Working out your GST turnover
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1793

Your GST turnover is your total business income (not your profit), minus:
GST included in sales to your customers
payments that aren't taxable (for example, gifts and donations to charities)
sales not connected with a business that you run (for example, selling your home or a personal asset)
input-taxed sales you make (for example, financial supplies and renting out residential premises)
sales not connected with Australia (for example, income received from offshore investments).
You reach the GST turnover threshold if either:
your current GST turnover (which is your turnover for the current month and the previous 11 months) totals $75,000 or more ($150,000 or more for non-profit organisations), or
your projected GST turnover (which is your turnover for the current month and the next 11 months) is likely to be $75,000 or more ($150,000 or more for non-profit organisations).


Monitoring your monthly turnover
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1794

You must monitor your total business income monthly to determine if you have reached or are likely to reach the GST turnover threshold. Once you reach the threshold, you have 21 days to register for GST.
It’s important to note that if you project or forecast that you will reach the GST turnover threshold in the next 12 months, you cannot wait until 12 months have passed to register for GST. You must register within 21 days of realising this will occur.

How do I register for GST?
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1795

If you’re required to register for GST, or want to voluntarily register, the easiest way to do this is online.
You can register online via:
ATO Online services for individuals and sole traders or
ATO Online services for business
You can also register:
by calling the ATO on 13 28 66
through your registered tax agent or BAS agent
using a paper form called ‘Add a new business account’. You can order the form using the online publication ordering service on the ATO website.
You will need an ABN before you can register for GST. Information on applying for an ABN can be found on the government’s Australian Business Register site.
The ATO does not charge a fee to register for an ABN or GST.
Each ABN requires a separate GST registration. However, if you have more than one business under the same ABN, you only need to register for GST once.
Let’s look at the following example.
Ivan is a sole trader who runs a small retail business selling clothes and accessories online and in a physical store. He also provides consulting services in the fashion industry. Although these are separate businesses, Ivan only needs to register for GST once as both businesses are both registered under the one ABN.
Once processed, the ATO will notify you of your GST registration details, including the date your registration is effective. From this date you will need to keep records for GST sales and purchases and will be required to lodge a BAS.
Be wary of third parties you don’t know offering to register for GST or an ABN on your behalf. They may not be legitimate, and you may end up being caught in a scam.
If you are uncertain or unsure, do not share any business or account details with them. Instead, speak to a registered tax adviser or contact the ATO.

What happens if I don’t register?
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1796

If you are required to register for GST but don’t, you will be required to pay GST from the date you were supposed to register.
This means you might need to send the ATO payments for GST, even though you didn’t collect it in your sales. You may also have to pay penalties and interest.
If you don't register for GST and you are supposed to, other businesses may be hesitant to do business with you, as it poses risks for them.
Not registering for and paying for GST when you are required to has a range of impacts on our community including reduced funding for hospitals, schools, roads, aged care and sporting facilities.
The ATO recognises the challenges that businesses face and are here to provide information and support you to do the right thing.
If at any point you realise that you should have registered for GST but haven’t, contact the ATO. They understand that occasionally genuine mistakes happen, and will work with you to fix the situation.

Scams, schemes and other fraudulent activities
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/goods-and-services-tax/scams-schemes-and-other-fraudulent-activities

As a business owner you need to be wary of potential scams, schemes or other fraudulent activities that could threaten your financial security and reputation.

Be aware of emails, texts or calls from people pretending to be from well-known businesses or government organisations (including the ATO).


You might be asked to provide personal or sensitive information which may enable the scammer to steal your identity, access your bank or tax records, set up a fake business in your name, or update your bank accounts to redirect payments to themselves.


Alternatively, you might be asked to make a payment that the scammer claims ‘you owe’.  


Scammers take advantage of people they see as ‘vulnerable’, which can include people experiencing illness or financial difficulties or people with disabilities. Be aware of anyone claiming to be a ‘friend’ who asks for your financial or personal details.

Scams

Keep an eye out for illegal tax schemes (particularly on social media) that promise ‘big gains’ in a short period of time, money being given away for free or loans that don’t need to be repaid.


If something seems too good to be true, it’s usually because it is.


Remember: The ATO does NOT offer loans. If you see someone advertising a way to get a loan from the ATO, it's not legitimate.

Schemes

Other fraudulent activities involve what is known as the ‘shadow economy’ (sometimes known as the ‘cash economy’).


Businesses engaging in the ‘shadow economy’ operate outside of our tax system.


For example - they may not record their cash sales or may pay workers ‘off the books’.


Under-reporting income and wages is a crime and businesses doing this are risking large fines, penalties, and their reputation. They’re also keeping money that would otherwise be used to support key services in our community.

GST and fraud

Fake businesses


The ATO has seen instances where an individual has set up a fake business and lodged activity statements to claim refunds for GST they were not entitled to. The business did not exist and the business owner had no documentation to support their claims for a GST refund.


Not only was their behaviour illegal, these individuals faced a range of penalties, were required to repay the money and in some cases, were jailed for up to 10 years.


Unreported cash sales


Data matching across a wide range of organisations and government agencies allows the ATO to identify businesses that are not reporting their cash sales, and therefore not paying their share of GST. Businesses who have done this have found themselves having to pay the ATO the unreported GST amounts and have faced heavy penalties and interest.

Types of sales
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/goods-and-services-tax/types-of-sales

Most goods and services supplied are classified into 3 categories, or type of sale:



	taxable sales

	GST-free sales

	input-taxed sales



Some sales are partly taxable. This means they are a mix of sales that attract GST and those that do not.

Sales of goods and services that must include GST in their price are known as ‘taxable sales’.


Most goods and services sold within Australia are considered ‘taxable sales’.


To be a ‘taxable sale’, a sale must be:



	for payment of some kind (for example, money or bartering)

	made in the course of operating your business (for example, goods or services that you are providing as part of your business)

	connected with Australia (this includes goods, property and other items).



If you’re unsure if the goods or services you provide are taxable, the following information on Taxable sales may help.

Taxable sales (GST sales)

Sales of goods and services which do not have GST included in the price are known as ‘GST-free sales’.


If the product or service you’re selling doesn’t attract GST, then you don’t include GST in its price.


The following sales are generally considered GST-free:



	basic food sales (for example, rice, eggs, and meat)

	some medical and health care services including some medicines

	some education and childcare services

	export sales

	sale of some telecommunication supplies

	precious metals sales.



This list doesn’t include all GST-free sales. Also, sometimes conditions apply which might mean the sale will attract GST. You can read more about GST-free sales on the ATO website.


Although you don’t include GST in the price of your GST-free sales, you can still claim credits for the GST included in the price of the purchases you’ve made to make your GST-free sales.


For example, a farmer grows potatoes and sells them at the produce markets. Potatoes are a basic food item and don’t attract GST. The farmer does not include GST in the price when he sells them.


However, the farmer can claim GST credits for the GST included in the price of purchases relating to growing potatoes, for example, fertiliser, fuel and freight.

GST-free sales

Some goods and services are sold without GST in their price, even though GST was included in the price of the ‘inputs’ used to make or supply them. These sales are known as input-taxed sales.


The most common input taxed sales are financial supplies (such as lending money or the provision of credit for a fee) and selling or renting out residential premises.


You don’t include GST in the price of your input taxed sales and you cannot claim credits for any GST you’ve paid when purchasing goods or services to make your input taxed sales.


Find more information about Input-taxed sales on the ATO website.

Input taxed sales

In some cases, you may have sales that are only partly taxable. This happens when your sale is made up of different components – some which attract GST and some which don’t. In this instance, you only add GST to the taxable components of the sale.

Partly taxable sales

Working out if your goods and services are taxable, GST-free or partly taxable can sometimes be tricky. If you’re uncertain, contact your registered tax agent use the ATO's Live chat service or call the ATO. They can provide accurate information that will help you get it right.


Calculating GST on sales
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1808

How do you calculate the goods and services tax for your sales which are subject to GST?
First you work out what your sale price (for example, material costs, labour, mark-up etc), then you add 10%.
Let’s look at an example.
Let’s assume you manufacture and sell running shoes, and you need to work out how much GST to charge on the latest pair of shoes.
First, work out the total cost base of the shoes (make sure you include the cost of materials, labour costs, markup, profit etc). This amount is your GST exclusive amount. In this instance, let’s say it's $160.
Next, calculate 10% of the GST exclusive amount. You do this by dividing the total cost base by 10.
For example: $160 divided by 10 = $16. This $16 is the GST amount.
To calculate the final price of your shoes, you add the total cost base amount and the GST amount together.
In this scenario, $160 + $16 = $176. This $176 is the total price of the running shoes (also known as the GST inclusive price).
A simple way to calculate your GST amounts is to use the MoneySmart.gov.au GST calculator . It’s fast, accurate, and user friendly.

What are GST credits?
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/goods-and-services-tax/what-are-gst-credits

If you’re registered for GST and you purchase something for your business that includes GST in the cost, you may be able to claim a GST credit for the GST amount paid. You do this when you lodge your BAS.

You must be registered for GST in order to claim a GST credit. If you are not registered for GST, you can’t claim these amounts.


You can claim a GST credit if:



	you plan to use your purchase fully or partly for your business

	the purchase is not an input-taxed sale

	the price you paid included GST – this means the business or person you bought it from must also be registered for GST

	you paid for the item, or are liable for its payment, and

	you have a tax invoice from the supplier (for purchases more than $82.50).



You cannot claim a GST credit for items you've purchased that are unrelated to your business (for example, for private or domestic use).


There is a 4 year time limit for claiming GST credits. After that you cannot claim a credit for the GST you’ve paid.


Always remember that in order to claim a GST credit, you must have evidence of your purchase in the form of a valid tax invoice for any purchases over $82.50.


There are special rules for some items, including motor vehicles. To learn more, see Special rules for specific GST credit claims and GST and motor vehicles.

Calculating GST on purchases
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1815

When you make a purchase, ask for a tax invoice. You must have a valid tax invoice to claim GST credits for purchases over $82.50.
The GST amount doesn’t need to be listed separately on the invoice, it’s enough for a business to provide a total amount and state ‘GST included’.
How do you calculate the GST portion of an invoice if it hasn’t been listed separately?
Firstly, don’t make the mistake of dividing the total price by 10. This is a common error.
This calculation is different to the one you make when you add 10% to your total cost base to get your GST inclusive figure.
If you have an invoice and the GST hasn’t been listed separately, you will need to work out how much GST you’ve been charged for your purchase. You do this by dividing the total price of the purchase by 11.
Total price divided by 11 = GST amount
For example, let’s say you purchase an item for your business and pay $352 including GST. The GST isn’t listed separately on the tax invoice, so you must calculate it yourself.
If you divide the total price of $352 by 11, you get $32.
$352 divided by 11 = $32 GST
This $32 is the GST amount that you have paid when you purchased the item. It is also the amount you can claim as a GST credit.
Alternatively, you can use the Moneysmart.gov.au GST calculator to do the calculation for you. Using the GST calculator will save you time and helps to reduce calculation errors.


Private versus business use (partial GST credits)
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1816

If you purchase goods or services that you intend to use for both your business and privately, you will need to apportion your claim. You can only claim a GST credit for the portion that relates to your business use.
Let’s look at some examples.
Haruto is a florist and runs his own business in the city. He purchases a laptop for $1,200 which includes $109.90 for GST. Haruto has a separate computer set-up at home which he keeps for personal use and his kids use for their schoolwork. Haruto plans to use the new laptop solely for his business, which is 100% of the time. In this case, Haruto can claim the full GST credit of $109.90.
Aisha is a full-time university student and hosts a successful video series on social media platforms, Instagram and YouTube. She has voluntarily registered for GST.
Aisha has a laptop which needs upgrading, so she purchases a new laptop for $1,200 which includes $109.90 for GST. Aisha plans on using her new laptop for university assignments, emails, and her work as a social media influencer. Aisha estimates that 80% of the time she will use the laptop for personal/private tasks (including uni) and 20% of the time for her video series. In this instance Aisha can only claim 20% of the GST amount included in the price of the laptop, which is $21.98.


GST credits and income tax deductions
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1817

When you claim a GST credit on your BAS it’s important to understand how this impacts deductions on your tax return.
If you’re registered for GST and want to claim a deduction for a business purchase in your tax return, make sure you only claim the GST exclusive cost of the item (for example, the amount you paid minus GST). You do not include the GST paid as you can claim this as a credit on your BAS.
Consider the following examples.
Muhammad is a builder and has recently upgraded some of his tools. The cost of the new tools are $1,650 which includes $150 GST. Muhammad is registered for GST and claims a $150 GST credit on his quarterly BAS. At the end of the financial year, Muhammad claims a business deduction on his tax return of $1,500 for the tools. Muhammad isn’t entitled to claim a business deduction of $1,650 as he has already claimed the GST component of $150 GST on his BAS.
If you're not entitled to claim a GST credit for your business purchase, then you can claim the full cost of the goods or services, including GST, as a deduction.
John sells pottery at local markets. He purchases a portable credit card and EFTPOS machine for $65 (including $5.91 GST) to assist him with sales. John is not registered or required to register for GST and is therefore not able to claim a credit for the $5.91 GST paid. Instead, John can claim the full price of $65 as a business tax deduction on his tax return.
Remember that the ATO is there to assist you with any questions you might have. If you’re uncertain about something, you can access their Live chat service or give them a call.

GST and imports
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/module/1323/take

GST is payable on most goods imported into Australia. These goods are known as ‘taxable importations’.
GST on taxable importations is payable by businesses and individuals, regardless of whether they are registered for GST.
If you are registered for GST and you import goods as part of your business, you may be able to claim a credit for the GST you’ve paid on those goods.
Paying GST on imported goods
GST on taxable importations is collected by the Department of Home Affairs . The amount of GST payable is 10% of the value of the taxable importation.
To calculate the value of a taxable importation, you add:
the customs value of the goods
any customs duty payable
the amount paid or payable to transport the goods to their place of consignment in Australia
the insurance cost for that transport
any wine tax payable.
In most cases, GST is payable before the goods are released by Home Affairs.
If you are importing goods into Australia as part of your business, you can find more information about, GST and imported goods and Common GST errors – importing or exporting goods and services , on the ATO website.


GST and exports
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1820

In most cases, exports of goods and services from Australia, are GST-free.
This means that you don’t need to include GST in the price. You can however claim GST credits for the purchases you’ve made to make your exported goods or services.
To be GST-free, goods must be exported from Australia within 60 days, after either receiving payment for the goods or issuing an invoice for the goods (whichever comes first).
Extensions to the 60 day period may be granted in some cases and can be applied for via the ATO.
Exports can be complex. More information about exports and GST can be found on the ATO website.

Your record-keeping responsibilities
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/module/1326/take

As a business owner, you are responsible for understanding and meeting your GST record-keeping obligations . This is the case even if you use a registered tax adviser, accountant, or bookkeeper to help you.
Benefits of record-keeping
Maintaining records that are accurate and complete, enables you to:
monitor whether your business is running at a profit or loss
keep track of money you owe and money owed to you
monitor your cash flow to help you to make payments on time
demonstrate your financial position to others such as lenders or other businesses
prepare and lodge your BAS and tax return
avoid penalties which may apply for failing to keep records.


What records do I need to keep for GST?
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1823

You must keep all records that show the income and expenses you’ve used to calculate and support your claims for GST credits. This includes all sales, tax invoices and other GST-related transactions, fees, expenses, wages and any other business costs.
You must also keep any other documents that record adjustments or explain a decision, or a calculation made for GST purposes.
If you don’t have the appropriate records to support your GST credit claims, then the ATO may disallow your claims.
Note that you don’t need a tax invoice to claim GST credits for taxable goods you import into Australia (as part of your business). However, you will need to have documents from the Department of Home Affairs showing the amount of GST you paid on those imports.


How long do I need to keep GST records?
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1824

Generally, you must keep your tax invoices and GST records for 5 years. This 5 year period starts from the date you prepared or obtained the record, or when you completed the transaction the record relates to.
There are some situations where you will need to keep your records longer than 5 years, including if your business owns capital assets that are subject to capital gains tax.
You should keep records long enough to cover the period of review (also known as the amendment period) for an assessment that uses information from the record.
The Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) requires companies to keep records for 7 years.
If you don't keep the right tax records, you may incur financial penalties, or be required to complete a record-keeping course (or both).

Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1825

Having a record-keeping system that will allow you to meet your tax and GST obligations is essential, and the ATO encourages you to put something in place that works for you, right from the start. This will save you time and effort later on.
Your record-keeping system can be digital or manual. The ATO is progressively moving towards digital reporting, so they recommend digital record-keeping where possible. The same record-keeping principles and practices apply, regardless of the system you choose.
The ATO’s Record-keeping evaluation tool can help you assess how well you’re keeping your business records.
The module ‘Protecting yourself and your business’ provides further information on keeping your records safe.
Additional information on managing your records and meeting your obligations can be found in our Record-keeping course for small businesses.
You can find other resources that may be useful such as, digital record-keeping , on the ATO website.

Choosing an accounting method
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/module/1333/take

There are 2 methods of accounting for GST:
a cash basis, and
a non-cash-basis (also known as accruals).
The method you choose will affect when you must report GST.


Accounting for GST on a cash basis
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1830

You can use the cash accounting method for GST if your business has an aggregated turnover of less than $10 million. If it’s over $10 million, you must use the non-cash (or accruals) method.
With cash accounting, you record GST on a BAS that covers the period when you actually receive or make a payment for your sales and purchases.
Zayn runs a food catering service and on 10 September he takes an order for $1,100 worth of food (which includes $100 GST). The customer has 30 days to pay for their order. There is no need for Zayn to account for GST at the time of the sale (10 September) as he’s yet to receive any payment.
On 1 October, the customer pays Zayn $1,100 for their order. It is on this date (1 October) that Zayn accounts for the $100 GST, as this is when he actually receives the payment.
Accounting on a cash basis also means you report your GST on the BAS that covers the period you receive or make payments.
Let’s look again at Zayn’s situation. Assuming that Zayn reports and lodges his BAS quarterly, he will report the GST amount of $100 in the October – December financial quarter as this is when he received payment. This is despite making the sale in the previous (July – September) financial quarter.
Benefits of cash accounting
The key benefit of using the cash accounting method is improved cash flow management. You only need to record and pay GST to the ATO when you actually receive the payment from your customers. Unlike the accruals method, you don’t make GST payments on invoices that have yet to be paid.
Cash accounting is a simple way to account and shows how much money you have on hand. It is also suited to smaller businesses that handle cash transactions.
You can find more information about choosing an accounting method for GST , on the ATO website.


Accounting for GST on a non-cash basis
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1831

Most larger businesses must use the non-cash accounting (also known as accruals) method, although smaller businesses can choose this option.
With the non-cash (accruals) method, you record GST on a BAS that covers the period you either:
receive a payment or issue a tax invoice before receiving payment (for a sale)
receive an invoice from your supplier before making a payment, or make a payment for a purchase.
In simple terms, accounting on a non-cash basis means you account for GST on the BAS that covers the period in which:
you have made a sale (even if you are still waiting to receive payment)
make a purchase (even if you haven’t paid for it yet).
Consider the following example.
Software provider, ‘ABC Security’ provides protective IT software to individuals for $30 a month. ABC Security is registered for GST and accounts for GST using the non-cash accounting (accruals) method. They issue their clients with an invoice for $30 on the first day of each month and clients have 28 days to pay the due amount.
As ABC Security account on an accruals basis, they record the sale including the GST at the time they issue the invoice, not when the payment is made.
They also report the sale on the BAS that covers the period they issue the invoice, regardless of whether payment has been made.
Benefits of non-cash accounting (accruals) method
This method suits businesses that don't get paid straight away as it helps track their true financial position (for example, what is owed to the business and what the business owes others). It’s also helpful when dealing with multiple contracts and large amounts of money.
You can find more information about choosing an accounting method for GST , on the ATO website.

GST reporting methods
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/module/1342/take

Simpler BAS
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1839

If your GST turnover is less than $10 million, you can use the ‘Simpler BAS’ reporting method.

Important! Although you only report total figures to the ATO, you still need to make sure you have all of your records and documentation to support your claims. If you don’t, the ATO may question, adjust or disallow your claim.

They will support you to fix it. It might be as simple as adjusting your next BAS or revising a previous one.


GST errors
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1845

If you make a mistake with your BAS calculations, it can result in what is known as a ‘GST error’. The error may result in you reporting or paying:
too much GST (a credit error) or
too little GST (a debit error).
Credit errors
Examples of a credit error include:
reporting a GST sale twice
overstating the GST on your sales.
Debit errors
Examples of a debit error include:
failing to include GST on a taxable sale
understating the GST on your sales (for example, reporting too little GST on sales)
overstating GST credits (for example, claiming GST credits for a purchase twice).


Correcting GST errors
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/goods-and-services-tax/correcting-gst-errors

If you make a mistake when reporting your GST on an activity statement, you may be able to correct the error on the next activity statement. Depending on the type of error, you might need to meet certain criteria.

If your error is a credit error, you have 4 years from the day you lodge your BAS to correct it.  


Example: Reporting GST on a sale twice


Mei is a sole trader and reports and pays her GST quarterly. When preparing her BAS for the October to December quarter, Mei realises she has made a mistake on her July to September BAS, by reporting GST on a sale twice. Instead of reporting GST on sales of $10,000, Mei reported $20,000.


On her July to September BAS, Mei reported the following amounts:


Total sales of $110,000


GST on sales $20,000 (instead of $10,000).


The $10,000 is considered a GST credit error. Mei can correct the error on her BAS for the October to December quarter.

Correcting credit errors

If you make a debit error, you can correct your mistake if the following conditions are met:



	the debit error is within the debit error time limit
	
		if your GST turnover is less than $20 million, you must correct the error within 18 months of the due date of the BAS in which the error was made

		if your GST turnover is $20 million or more, you must correct the error within 12 months of the due date of the BAS in which the error was made.

	
	

	the net sum of the debit error is within the debit error value limit (the total error amount must be below a certain limit. This limit varies depending on your GST turnover)

	the debit error is a genuine mistake and has not been made recklessly or intentionally.



Example: Debit error


XYZ Ltd is a small company with a GST turnover of less than $2 million, that lodges their BAS monthly.


When preparing their BAS for the March period they realise they had forgotten to include $1,000 GST collected on their previous BAS (February period).


XYZ reported the following on their BAS for the month of February:



	Total sales of $121,000

	GST collected $10,000 (this should have been $11,000)

	GST credits $5,000.



This mistake has resulted in a debit error as XYZ Ltd has accidently understated the GST they collected and should have paid the ATO. They reported $10,000 GST collected instead of $11,000.


XYZ Ltd can correct this error as long as the correction is made within 18 months of the due date of the BAS which had the error, and it was a genuine mistake that was not been made recklessly or intentionally.

Correcting debit errors

You can find more information about correcting GST errors on the ATO website.


Whilst the ATO supports business owners who make genuine errors or mistakes, there are serious consequences for those who deliberately misreport their GST amounts. These include harsh fines and penalties and, in some cases, jail.


GST adjustments
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1847

You may need to make an adjustment if you reported a GST sale or purchase on your previous BAS, but something occurs after you’ve lodged that changes the amount of GST you’ve reported or an amount of GST credit claimed.
For example, Matt and Joel own a hardware store, are registered for GST and lodge and pay their GST monthly. In January, they sell a lawn mower for $1,200. The lawn mower is a taxable supply and Matt (who does the books) includes $120 GST in the total GST sales amount on the January BAS.
In early February, the customer returns the lawn mower to the store (under a 3 month ‘buy and try’ warranty) and asks for their money back. Joel processes the refund of $1,200 for the customer.
As the sale was refunded, Matt will need to make an adjustment, effectively removing the $1,200 sale and $120 GST from the January activity statement. Matt does this by making an adjustment in his February activity statement.
You can make a GST adjustment on your BAS using:
ATO Online services for individuals
ATO Online services for business
Standard Business Reporting (SBR) enabled software
your registered tax adviser or BAS agent
requesting a paper revision form.

Getting support for lodging and paying on time
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/module/1353/take

If you’re having difficulty lodging your BAS and paying your GST on time, don’t wait until after the due date for lodgment. There are a number of support options you can access to meet your tax and super obligations.
For help budgeting for tax, preventing debt and setting up a payment play, see Help with paying on the ATO website.
You can also call the ATO on 13 11 42 to discuss new or existing debts, payment arrangements, overdue lodgments and penalties and interest.
Payment plans
If you have a GST debt you can’t pay in full or on time you may be able to enter into a payment plan. A payment plan allows you to pay small amounts (agreed to by the ATO and you) either weekly, fortnightly or monthly, over a specified time, until your debt is cleared.
The Payment plan estimator can help you work out how soon you can pay off your debt, what your payment amounts might look like, and how much interest you will pay.
Free financial counselling services
Financial counsellors provide information, support and advocacy to assist people in financial difficulty. Their services are free, independent and confidential.
The National Debt Helpline (NDH) is an online and phone financial counselling service available across Australia. NDH counsellors can explain what options are available to you if you find yourself in debt.
Remember: If you are struggling or concerned that you may not be able to meet your GST lodgment and payment obligations, contact the ATO. Ignoring the situation will only lead to a higher debt and penalties for failing to lodge.


Contacting the ATO for support
Source: https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/group/360/learning-path/nextstep/1850

The ATO recognises the challenges that businesses face, particularly small businesses.
The ATO website www.ato.gov.au contains a range of information and tools to help you set up, operate, and manage your business. This includes a live chat service (10:00 am – 6:00 pm AEST, Monday – Friday).
The ATO also have trained staff who can provide unbiased and accurate information to help you get your GST right and to protect you from those seeking to take advantage of you. If you’re unable to find what you need on the website, you can call the ‘Business tax’ line on 13 28 66 (8:00 am – 6:00 pm AEST, Monday - Friday).
If you require additional information on new or existing debts, payment plans, overdue lodgments and penalties and interest, you can call the ATO ‘Lodge and pay’ line on 13 11 42 .
Remember: If you having difficulty meeting your GST or other tax obligations, contact the ATO. They are there to support you.
